**`L-lunar-material-delivered-to-orbit-at-scale`**
The dependency tree assumes the hardest foundational barriers are already cleared: surface power runs through the lunar night, heavy reusable transport routinely reaches the Moon, and political continuity sustains the program across decades. Once those physical and political foundations exist, lifting a hundred tonnes requires operational cadence rather than a fundamental breakthrough. A hundred tonnes equates to only a handful of flights on a heavy reusable lander architecture. If the infrastructure is funded and functional, the sheer economic gravity of orbital manufacturing will pull that material up rapidly.

**`L-launch-price-below-200-dollars-per-kg`**
Price tracks marginal cost once a technology achieves scale and amortizes its initial research and development. A fully reusable heavy lift vehicle flying a hundred times a year—the explicit dependency for this node—creates a vast, unprecedented oversupply of orbital tonnage. A monopoly premium cannot hold for forty-five years against rival private aerospace firms and national programs operating in the same domain. If the dependency of a hundred annual flights resolves, competitive market pressure and operational rhythm will compress the sale price below two hundred dollars long before the century closes.

**`D-nuclear-propulsion-in-routine-use`**
The primary barrier to nuclear propulsion is the initial demonstration flight, which demands overcoming immense regulatory friction, launch safety reviews, and novel integration engineering. The jump from one successful flight to routine use is merely an adoption problem. Once a nuclear system operates in space and proves its specific impulse advantage, the transit-time mathematics for deep space and Mars missions aggressively favor it over chemical alternatives. Success on the first flight unlocks immediate institutional demand, pulling routine use forward rapidly.
