---

**The Belt Equation is one of the more honest personal forecasting projects I’ve seen.** It turns a private dream—“I want to work off Earth before I die”—into a public, inspectable probability tree of roughly 60 nodes under eight factors (Launch, Energy, Drive, Biology, Motive, Regime, Access, plus a non-multiplied Contact clause). The headline number on the site as of the 2026-09-19/20 run is **0.9% by 2071** for the author (born January 1986) to work on months-long rotations off Earth. That is low, and the site is explicit about why.

### What the equation actually is
It is not a single closed-form formula. It is a dependency-ordered Monte Carlo simulation (20,000 play-throughs per longevity scenario). Each node has:
- a clear resolution criterion,
- the nodes it depends on,
- five conditional probabilities (2071 / 2080 / 2095 / 2136 / open-ended),
- and the rule that probabilities may not fall as the window lengthens.

The seven factors that *are* multiplied are the ones required for the “Slow Expanse” tier (T3): cheap reusable launch + surface power + routine nuclear propulsion + permanent site beyond the Earth-Moon system + closed-loop life support + economic motive + political continuity + a seat for the individual. The product of those conditionals, after the tree has enforced the dependencies, lands at ~0.9% under baseline longevity. Longer windows raise it, as expected.

The site is transparent about methodology, shows what each individual milestone is currently “worth” to the headline, keeps a public changelog, and invites disagreement. That is rarer than it should be.

### Strengths
- **Conditional probabilities and dependency order** are the right way to avoid double-counting and optimism bias. Most space forecasts fail here.
- **Resolution criteria are mostly public and falsifiable.** That is better than the usual “by 2040 we will have X” claims that never define what counts.
- **Longevity is treated as a dial**, not a fixed assumption. Running five scenarios and forbidding falling probabilities is clean.
- **Personal agency is isolated.** Only the Access factor is under the author’s control; everything else is treated as the external world. The project is therefore both a forecast and a decision tool for the remaining choices (training, career path).
- **Public calibration intent.** The author plans yearly re-checks and a calibration score. If that discipline holds, the tree will improve over time in a measurable way.

### Weaknesses and places the numbers look too high
The packet I reviewed earlier (Launch, Energy, Drive) already showed several optimistic conditionals. Looking at the full site confirms the pattern continues:

- **Launch** is still the most optimistic block. Full reuse of both stages is treated as nearly certain in the near term, yet as of September 2026 Starship has only achieved ocean recovery of a non-reusable Ship. The step from “both stages recovered intact once” to “100 orbital flights of a fully reusable vehicle in a calendar year” and then to sustained public pricing below $200/kg (2026 dollars) is larger than the assigned probabilities imply. The tree’s sensitivity numbers already show that failure of the cheap-launch chain zeros the headline; the conditional probabilities on those nodes therefore matter a lot and currently look high.
- **Drive** underweights the distance from “first nuclear propulsion burn that changes orbit” to “routine use on several missions a year.” Sixty years of ground work and recent cancellations are real history; new DoD/NASA funding (Antares, SR-1 Freedom) is real too, but the gap remains large.
- **Motive** (6.8% by 2071) is correctly identified by the site as the weakest link. A profitable off-Earth product plus a hundred people working for pay is still a major economic and institutional hurdle even after the technical pieces exist. The tree is right to flag it; I would not raise it.
- **Cross-factor dependencies** are present but incomplete. Lunar surface fission, propellant transfer at scale, and closed-loop life support interact more tightly than a pure tree sometimes captures. A single major failure (e.g., sustained high-rate cryogenic transfer never working cleanly) cascades further than the current edges show.
- **Biology and Regime** are the two I have not deep-dived node-by-node, but the same pattern is likely: clear criteria, publicly checkable sources of truth, and conditionals that are somewhat optimistic once the full set of real-world failure modes (radiation, microgravity developmental effects, political continuity across multiple administrations and recessions) is considered.

The constant-acceleration “Epstein” drive node is correctly treated as a long shot and is *not* required for the headline (it sits under T4 Full Expanse). That is good discipline.

### Overall judgment
The project is unusually rigorous for a personal forecast. The structure—dependency tree, conditional probabilities, public resolution criteria, Monte Carlo aggregation, longevity dial, and invitation to challenge—is better than most professional space-settlement forecasts I have seen. The headline number being low is a feature, not a bug; multiplying seven imperfect links really does produce small products.

Where I disagree is mostly on the *level* of several near- and mid-term conditionals in Launch, Energy, and Drive (the ones I reviewed earlier). Lowering those would push the 2071 headline further down, probably into the 0.3–0.6% range under the same tree structure. Longer windows would still raise it, and the relative ranking of what moves the number (cheap launch and biology first) would likely stay intact.

It is also refreshingly non-hype. The author is not selling a vision; he is trying to measure the distance to one and to identify the levers he still controls. That alone makes the site worth watching. The numbers will move as nodes resolve or as outside critiques (including the ones already submitted) are absorbed. The fact that the tree is public and the computation is reproducible is the strongest reason to take it seriously.
