I've started keeping a number, and I'd like you to argue with it.

The number is the probability that I get to live and work off Earth before I die. Not "humanity," me, with my actual age and my actual life expectancy. The project's name comes from The Expanse, where the people who live out in the asteroid belt are called Belters. The question, exactly as I've written it down, is: what is the probability that, before I die, I live and work in a solar system where human industry reaches beyond Earth and Mars?

The way I'm answering it is borrowed from a 1961 astronomer named Frank Drake, who wanted to estimate how many alien civilizations might be out there and realized nobody could answer that, but everybody could argue about seven smaller numbers, and if you multiply them together you get your answer. That's the Drake Equation, and the reason it's famous has nothing to do with the estimate: it turned an impossible question into a dinner argument.

So here's mine. Eight things have to go right, and each one gets a probability.

I have to still be alive. Launch has to get cheap enough that putting people and things in space stops being the expensive part. There has to be real power out there, starting with a reactor on the Moon. There has to be a way to move around the solar system in months instead of years. Human bodies have to be able to live out there, and eventually have kids out there, or we have to build spinning stations that fake gravity well enough. There has to be a reason for a lot of people to be there, meaning someone is making money. The politics have to hold together for decades without a war or a budget collapse killing it. And I personally need a way in, given my job, my health, and my training.

Multiply those together and you get a small number. Right now my best estimate is 0.4 percent that I work off Earth on rotation, the way people work on oil platforms or in Antarctica, in a solar system where human industry reaches beyond Earth and Mars, before I die. That's if I live to about 2071, which is roughly what the tables say for a man my age. It's 1.1 percent if medicine gives me an extra decade, 3.5 percent if it gives me until 2095, and 14 percent if aging gets solved and I'm around to see the whole thing. If you'd settle for me doing a stint at a station or a Moon base before industry reaches any further, that's a nearer thing, about 6 percent by 2071, and it's on the site next to the main number so nobody can accuse me of hiding it.

Small numbers are the point. Every one of those eight things gets broken down into a list of specific events I can check, things like "a rocket brings both of its stages back from one orbital flight, ready to fly again" or "a mammal gets conceived, born, and weaned in Moon-strength gravity." There are sixty-nine of those, and a third of them will resolve one way or the other in the next five years. So every year I go back, mark what happened, move the numbers, and see whether the big number went up or down. The number moving is the product, and the number itself is only a snapshot.

I didn't trust my own numbers, so before I sent this I had two other AI systems, Google's and OpenAI's, go through every one of them with instructions to argue. They found seven things I'd left off the list entirely, like somebody insuring a mine on the Moon and somebody performing surgery out there, and the number fell from 0.9 percent to 0.4 once those went in. All hundred and five of their objections and my answers are public.

Two things I'll say up front so you don't have to. Yes, some of the items on the list are 99.9 percent dreaming. They stay on the list on purpose, because they're what make the dream vivid enough to do anything about, and every one of them has to be written so that each step toward it is plausible, the way The Expanse makes its wildest turns feel like something that could happen. And no, I'm not designing my life around this. The list tells me which small decisions in the next couple of years put me on the most interesting paths, and that's worth more to me than the final number.

What I want from you: tell me which of the eight you think I've got wrong, and by how much. Tell me what's missing from the list. If you think the whole thing is silly, tell me that too, but you have to give me a number.

It's all at beltequation.com, and the list changes every time somebody convinces me.
