# The founding inspiration: "Home" (The Expanse, Season 2, Episode 5)

Written 2026-09-08. This is the scene John names as the pinnacle of the dream in its most beautiful form, and the one he intends to keep returning to as he writes about the project. This note is the public reference for it. Clip: "The Expanse - Home Ending," https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TPA8M7kZc2g (6 min 29 s). The episode is the fifth of the second season, first aired 2017-03-01.

## What happens

Eros, a Belter asteroid station whose population has been consumed by the protomolecule, an alien technology of unknown origin, has begun moving under its own power and is falling toward Earth. Nothing humanity has can stop it. Detective Josephus Miller, a Belter who spent the first season searching for a missing young woman named Julie Mao, is on the surface of Eros with a nuclear device, meaning to detonate it by hand.

He goes inside instead. In the heart of the station he finds what the protomolecule has made of Julie: she is still herself, still frightened, dreaming of going home, and it is her mind, joined with the alien machinery, that has been steering the rock toward Earth. Miller does not fight her and does not use the bomb. He sits with her, talks her through what has happened, and asks her to choose somewhere else. Together they turn Eros away from Earth and fly it into Venus, where the protomolecule can build whatever it is going to build without killing anyone. Miller stays with her to the end. The last image is the two of them, hand in hand, going home.

## Why it is the pinnacle of the dream

The scene contains every layer of this project at once.

It is a Belter story. Julie was a rich Earther who chose the Belt and its cause; Miller is a Belter cop who was never good at belonging anywhere. Eros was a Belter station, and its people were the ones the Inners used as test subjects. The whole tragedy is the Belt paying for the ambitions of others, and the two people who end it are Belters who refuse the roles they were handed. That refusal is the spirit John wants the project to carry: the number may be small, and the Belt still gets built by people who decided to be there anyway.

It is contact. The protomolecule is a mind, or something that behaves like one, of non-human origin, acting in ways that defy the physics we know, and the scene shows what communion with such a thing might look like: not a message decoded, but a person understood. Julie, joined to it, reaches something greater than human science could have produced, and Miller reaches her. This is Contact Clause rung C4 in dramatic form, and the reason the Clause exists in the project at all.

It is plausible all the way to the edge of the impossible. Nothing in the scene asks the viewer to stop believing. The physics of the station, the bomb, the burn, the fear, the exhaustion, all of it is grounded, so that when the impossible thing happens it feels like something that could occur in the real solar system. This is the second founding rule of the tree, stated in docs/definitions.md: a long-shot node must be written so that each step toward it is believable in sequence, with the mechanism named and the breaking point stated plainly.

And it is a story about choosing where to go. Julie has enormous power and no idea what to do with it; she is heading for the wrong place because it is the only place she can picture. What Miller gives her is a better destination and someone to go there with. The project's own purpose, deciding which near-term choices put a person on the most interesting path, is a very small version of the same act.

## How the scene maps onto the project

- The Belt as a place people live and die for: system Tiers 3 and 4, and the reason the headline tier is A2 (working out there on rotation) rather than a single flight.
- The protomolecule as non-human intelligence: Contact Clause rungs C3 (an intelligence not descended from us is detected) and C4 (communion).
- Julie and Miller's refusal: the founding rule that long shots stay on the board, and the Belter register of the question's wording.
- The groundedness of the impossible: the plausibility rule for writing long-shot nodes.
- Choosing a destination: the choice-point nodes in the access branch and the run-per-option method.
