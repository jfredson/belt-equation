The [European Space Agency](https://www.esa.int/)'s [Hera](https://www.esa.int/missions/about-us/directorates/operations/space-safety/hera) spacecraft has [arrived at its target asteroids](https://www.esa.int/articles/about-us/directorates/operations/space-safety/hera/hera-spacecraft-in-rectangular-orbit-around-target-asteroids), a pair that includes the one NASA struck on purpose in 2022, and has begun its first survey of them.

## What happened

In September 2022, NASA flew its [DART spacecraft](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/dart/) into a small asteroid called [Dimorphos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimorphos), to see whether hitting an asteroid could change its path. Dimorphos is a moonlet: it circles a larger asteroid, [Didymos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/65803_Didymos). The hit worked. ESA's release gives the figures: a [580-kilogram spacecraft at 6.1 kilometres a second](https://www.esa.int/articles/about-us/directorates/operations/space-safety/hera/hera-spacecraft-in-rectangular-orbit-around-target-asteroids) changed the time Dimorphos takes to go around Didymos by about 33 minutes, and threw out a plume of debris that spread across 10,000 kilometres. For scale, Dimorphos takes [about 12 hours](https://skyandtelescope.org/astronomy-news/spacecraft-investigates-asteroid-after-impact/) to go around Didymos.

Hera was built to go and look at what that hit left behind. It [launched on October 7th, 2024](https://www.esa.int/missions/about-us/directorates/operations/space-safety/hera), and it [met the two asteroids on November 11th, 2026](https://skyandtelescope.org/astronomy-news/spacecraft-investigates-asteroid-after-impact/), the day ESA announced its arrival.

## How you fly around something this small

Didymos is [about 780 metres across, and Dimorphos about 151 metres](https://www.esa.int/articles/about-us/directorates/operations/space-safety/hera/hera-spacecraft-in-rectangular-orbit-around-target-asteroids). Their gravity is so weak, about 40,000 times weaker than Earth's on Didymos and 200,000 times weaker on Dimorphos, that ESA calls an ordinary circular orbit around them impractical. Instead it drifts past them in nearly straight lines at about 12 centimetres a second, which ESA likens to a fast-crawling insect, and fires its thrusters every few days to turn a corner of 90 degrees. The result is a path shaped like a rectangle that keeps it 25 to 30 kilometres from the pair.

ESA says the pattern is safe because each firing comes while Hera is moving away from the asteroids, so a firing that fails leaves it drifting away from them, not into them. Flight controllers in Darmstadt, in Germany, talk to it [about eight hours a day](https://www.esa.int/articles/about-us/directorates/operations/space-safety/hera/hera-spacecraft-in-rectangular-orbit-around-target-asteroids), across a gap that was 196.5 million kilometres on the day it arrived, through large dish antennas in Argentina, Spain and Australia.

I follow Hera because flying safely near a body with almost no gravity is one of the skills that any work at an asteroid will need.

## What it has seen so far

The first pictures show mostly Didymos. One taken [from 56 kilometres away](https://www.esa.int/articles/about-us/directorates/operations/space-safety/hera/hera-spies-a-half-hidden-asteroid-moonlet) shows large boulders, smoother ground and a sharp ridge around its middle, with only a small part of Dimorphos peeking out from behind it. Didymos spins once every 2.2 hours, close to fast enough to come apart, and ESA notes that loose material may be flowing toward its equator, where the ridge is, as a result. Some of it may even have been thrown off over time, which could be how Dimorphos came to exist.

ESA is holding back its first clear look at Dimorphos, the asteroid DART struck, for [an event in Berlin on November 25th](https://skyandtelescope.org/astronomy-news/spacecraft-investigates-asteroid-after-impact/).

## Why it matters

Hitting an asteroid to nudge it aside is one way people might one day protect Earth, but it is not yet understood well enough to rely on. ESA lists [what is still unknown](https://www.esa.int/articles/about-us/directorates/operations/space-safety/hera/hera-spacecraft-in-rectangular-orbit-around-target-asteroids): whether DART left a crater or reshaped Dimorphos entirely, what the asteroid is made of and how much it weighs, and which of its properties explain why the push was so much larger than computer simulations of the impact had predicted. "Previous predictions by our impact models suggested many different ways the asteroid body might react, but Hera will reveal which forecast was the correct one – if any!" said Michael Kueppers, Hera's mission scientist, in ESA's release.

## What to watch for

First, on [November 25th](https://www.esa.int/articles/about-us/directorates/operations/space-safety/hera/hera-spies-a-half-hidden-asteroid-moonlet), come the first detailed pictures of Dimorphos. After that, Hera keeps this distance [until about December 2nd](https://skyandtelescope.org/astronomy-news/spacecraft-investigates-asteroid-after-impact/) and then moves in to between 8 and 20 kilometres for a closer survey. Over the months that follow it is due to [release two small satellites, each about the size of a shoebox](https://www.esa.int/articles/about-us/directorates/operations/space-safety/hera/hera-spacecraft-in-rectangular-orbit-around-target-asteroids), to look closer still. ESA says the mission is to end with a landing.
