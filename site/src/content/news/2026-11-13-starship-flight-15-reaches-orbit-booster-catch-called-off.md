*Rewritten on November 14th, 2026, after [the upper stage came home](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/spacex-catch-starship-rocket/). This post first went up on launch night as a report of the flight. It now leads with why the catch matters, and the flight itself is told in one short section near the end.*

Every rocket that has ever reached orbit has thrown part of itself away, and [SpaceX](https://www.spacex.com/)'s giant [Starship](https://www.spacex.com/vehicles/starship/) did so again on its fifteenth flight, when its booster was lost over the Gulf of Mexico. But on November 14th, 2026, the launch tower in South Texas [caught the other half](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/spacex-catch-starship-rocket/), the upper stage, as it came home from orbit about 22 hours after it left. That stage was the last part of the rocket still discarded on every flight. If it can be caught, checked and sent up again within days, then the price of reaching orbit stops being the price of building a new rocket, and every plan for working beyond Earth starts from that lower price.

## The part that was always thrown away

Think of an airline that built a new aeroplane for every trip. Fuel and crews would be a rounding error beside the cost of the plane, and tickets would cost what an aeroplane costs. Rockets have mostly worked that way. SpaceX broke half of the pattern with [Falcon 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9), its workhorse rocket, whose booster has landed and flown again routinely since 2018. Reuse is a large part of why its price fell, and [Wikipedia's table of launch prices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_launch_market_competition) now puts a Falcon 9 at about $2,720 for each kilogram delivered to low orbit. But the other half never came back. SpaceX's factory still [builds a new second stage for every Falcon 9 launch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9), and each one flies only once.

Starship was designed so that both halves come home. [If it works as designed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starship), it will be the first orbital rocket that is fully reusable, and SpaceX says its aim is to cut launch costs by reusing both stages and building them in large numbers. The booster half has been caught at the tower before. The upper stage is the harder half to bring back, because it returns from orbit, falling into the atmosphere far faster than the booster ever travels, and until Saturday no Starship upper stage had been recovered.

## From one catch to an economy beyond Earth

[The Belt Equation](https://beltequation.com/) is my public estimate of one question: what is the chance that, before I die, I live and work in a solar system where human industry reaches beyond Earth and Mars? It breaks that question into a tree of concrete events, each with its own probability, and computes the answer from the tree instead of choosing it by hand. Today the answer is [0.465 percent by 2071](https://beltequation.com/). Launch cost sits at the bottom of the tree, and the steps above this catch run in a chain. Each figure below is my estimate of the chance the step happens by 2071 if the step before it has already happened:

1. [Both halves of a heavy rocket come back from one flight to orbit](https://beltequation.com/tree/launch/L-fully-reusable-heavy-launcher-recovers-both-stages/), and the company confirms both can fly again: 95 percent. This catch is one half of that.
2. [A fully reusable heavy rocket flies a hundred times in one year](https://beltequation.com/tree/launch/L-heavy-launcher-100-per-year/), with both halves reused: 85 percent.
3. [Mass to low orbit is sold for under $200 a kilogram](https://beltequation.com/tree/launch/L-launch-price-below-200-dollars-per-kg/): 60 percent. That is about a fourteenth of the Falcon 9 price, and at that price a tonne in orbit costs $200,000, which is about the price of a house.
4. [A hundred people work off Earth at the same time, for pay](https://beltequation.com/tree/motive/M-hundred-people-working-off-earth-for-pay/), every month for a year: 50 percent. This needs the hundred-flight year, and it needs space budgets to recover after the next recession.

That fourth step is the bottom rung of the tree's ladder of outcomes, a permanent outpost. Every rung above it, from thousands of people living off Earth up to tens of thousands with work of their own out there, requires the cheap price in step three. So the launch link holds up every rung of the ladder, and that is why one catch matters to the whole equation.

It is also, by my own numbers, one of the healthiest links in the chain. The tightest one is [something mined or made off Earth sold at a profit](https://beltequation.com/tree/motive/M-off-earth-product-sold-at-a-profit/), which I put at about 6 percent by 2071 once everything under it is counted, as I set out in [*The Belt Equation*](https://sentient-horizons.com/essays/the-belt-equation/), the essay that introduced the project. Cheap launch is what lets anyone try to make money out there; it does not make the money. The catch strengthens the bottom of the ladder, and the question the equation is waiting on sits several rungs up.

## What the catch did to the numbers

Nothing in the tree moved, and I think that is right. The step the catch bears on most directly, [both halves back from one flight](https://beltequation.com/tree/launch/L-fully-reusable-heavy-launcher-recovers-both-stages/), is not met, because the booster was lost and SpaceX has not said whether this upper stage will fly again. Before the flight I already had that step at 95 percent by 2071, on the reasoning that the risk left was the programme being cancelled and not the physics of catching the stage. A catch of the harder half bears that reasoning out, and it adds little to a number already that high.

The weekly scan on the [Radar page](https://beltequation.com/radar/), which reads the tree's near-term steps against the week's news every Sunday, last ran on November 8th, before launch, and recorded the flight as a plan. The next scan, on November 15th, will read the catch against the tree, and anything that moves will show there with its reason.

## What the catch says to the Quiet Galaxy

[The Quiet Galaxy](https://thequietgalaxy.com/) is the Belt Equation's sibling, a public tracker of the [Fermi Paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi_paradox): the puzzle of why, in a galaxy this old and this large, no one else has shown up. Beside its estimate it keeps a catalogue of every serious explanation for the silence, and several of them turn on one question about any civilisation that learns to leave its planet, which is whether it keeps going.

- [Interstellar travel is impractical](https://thequietgalaxy.com/explanations/interstellar-travel-impractical/). The astronomer Sebastian von Hoerner worked through the limits of space travel in 1962 and concluded that it would stay within our own solar system. One reading of the silence is that every civilisation looks at the cost, as we do, and decides it is not worth it.
- [Expansion stalls](https://thequietgalaxy.com/explanations/expansion-stalls/). The NASA scientist and author Geoffrey Landis argued in 1998 that if each new settlement has only some chance of sending out settlers of its own, the spread can fizzle or leave large regions unreached. The page names Earth's own future settlement of the solar system, and whether its daughter settlements keep expanding, as evidence that would strengthen the idea.
- [The inward turn](https://thequietgalaxy.com/explanations/inward-turn/). The psychologist Geoffrey Miller suggested in 2006 that civilisations lose interest in the slow, hard work of exploring space as their entertainment gets better. The page lists humanity's continued investment in exploration alongside ever better entertainment as evidence against it.

In [*The Quiet Galaxy Hypothesis*](https://sentient-horizons.com/essays/the-quiet-galaxy-hypothesis-advanced-intelligence-informational-resilience-and-the-ethics-of-cosmic-silence/) I argued that civilisations which last long enough tend to go quiet, turning from outward growth toward inward refinement, so that a galaxy full of old civilisations would look empty from here. That turn comes late, after an outward phase, and nothing in the argument says a young civilisation does not reach out first. Our own civilisation is the only one anyone can watch from the inside, and each of these explanations makes a claim about what a civilisation does once leaving gets cheap. Until now, anyone could fairly say that we have not spread out because it costs too much. A rocket that comes home whole would turn "we cannot afford to go" into "we chose to go, or chose not to", which is the form of the question these pages ask. If launch gets cheap, the Belt Equation will record over the coming decades whether we keep going, and that record is one data point the Quiet Galaxy can read.

None of this moves a number there either. The Quiet Galaxy's estimate counts civilisations and how long they stay detectable, and these explanations bear on why no one has visited, which the count leaves open. A rocket to low orbit is also a very long way from a ship to another star.

## What the catch does not show yet

The booster was lost. It [called off its own catch](https://www.space.com/space-exploration/launches-spacecraft/spacex-starship-flight-15-chopsticks-double-catch-super-heavy-ship) about six and a half minutes into the flight, after the burn that turns it back toward the tower ended early on what Kate Tice of SpaceX called, on the company's broadcast, [a "low fuel mass alarm"](https://www.space.com/news/live/spacex-starship-flight-15-live-updates-nov-14-2026-starship-first-double-landing-attempt). It [exploded moments before it reached the water](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/spacex-catch-starship-rocket/). This was the first try at catching the newest version of the booster. SpaceX's tower [caught three boosters of the first version](https://www.spacex.com/launches/starship-flight-15) on earlier flights, and two of those flew again.

A catch is also not a reflight. [Gwynne Shotwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwynne_Shotwell), SpaceX's president, said on the company's earnings call of October 20th, 2026 that it aims to ["refly the second stage by early next year"](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/10/20/spacex-spcx-q3-2026-earnings-call-transcript/), and that is the event the cost argument depends on. It may not be this stage. [Elon Musk has said](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/spacex-catch-starship-rocket/), as CBS News reports, that the early ships and boosters are already outdated by better designs in production, so the first upper stage to fly twice may be a later one.

And coming back has been done before without making launch cheap. NASA's five [Space Shuttle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle) orbiters returned from orbit and flew again across 135 missions between 1981 and 2011. Yet at the end of the programme NASA put the cost of each launch at about $450 million. Between flights the orbiter's main engines were taken out and replaced, and the huge fuel tank was thrown away every time. A stage that comes home and then has to be partly rebuilt saves far less than it seems to. So the claim that holds is narrower than "the first reusable upper stage": this was the first tower catch of an upper stage built to be flown again quickly. Musk said on the October call that the goal is to [land the rocket and launch it again "just like an airplane"](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/10/20/spacex-spcx-q3-2026-earnings-call-transcript/), and that the first reuses will still need some refurbishment.

The hopeful reading rests on the record. The Falcon 9 booster first landed [in December 2015](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9), and by 2018 landing and reflying it was routine, which is a large part of why [its price fell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_launch_market_competition). Nothing guarantees that the upper stage follows the same path. If it does, though, November 14th, 2026 is to the second half of the rocket what that first landing was to the first half: the day the thing that was always thrown away came home for the first time.

## The flight, in short

Starship lifted off from Pad 2 at [Starbase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starbase), SpaceX's site in South Texas, at [4:47 p.m. Texas time on November 13th, 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_flight_15), with Booster 22 underneath and Ship 42, the upper stage, on top. After the booster turned back and was lost, the ship reached orbit, released 26 of SpaceX's newest [Starlink](https://www.starlink.com/) internet satellites and [circled the Earth 14 times](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/14/spacex-plans-to-try-first-ever-catch-of-starship-rocket-from-orbit.html). Then it fired one engine to slow down, fell back through the atmosphere and flew to the tower it had left the day before. The tower's arms caught it at [about 3:20 p.m. Texas time on November 14th](https://www.space.com/news/live/spacex-starship-flight-15-live-updates-nov-14-2026-starship-first-double-landing-attempt), about 22 and a half hours after liftoff. "We caught a Starship, and now everything changes," Dan Huot of SpaceX said on the broadcast.

## What to watch next

- **Whether a caught upper stage flies again**, and how much work it needs first. SpaceX's [target is early 2027](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/10/20/spacex-spcx-q3-2026-earnings-call-transcript/). A quick turnaround with a check and fresh fuel is what would make the cost argument real.
- **The first flight that brings both halves home**, once the cause of the booster's fuel alarm is known. With SpaceX confirming both can fly again, that flight would meet [the first step in the chain](https://beltequation.com/tree/launch/L-fully-reusable-heavy-launcher-recovers-both-stages/).
- **How often Starship flies.** It had flown [three times in 2026](https://beltequation.com/radar/) before this flight. Musk said on the October call that he expects weekly flights [at some point in the first half of 2027](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/10/20/spacex-spcx-q3-2026-earnings-call-transcript/), and Starship's dates have slipped before: the first try at catching an upper stage was [planned for August 2026](https://beltequation.com/tree/launch/L-fully-reusable-heavy-launcher-recovers-both-stages/) and came in November. The hundred-flight year is the second step.
- **The first attempt to refill a Starship in orbit**, which Shotwell also [targeted for early 2027](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/10/20/spacex-spcx-q3-2026-earnings-call-transcript/). The tree's Moon steps [depend on refilling at a far larger scale](https://beltequation.com/tree/launch/L-orbital-propellant-transfer-at-scale/), a hundred tonnes of fuel pumped from one spacecraft to another.
